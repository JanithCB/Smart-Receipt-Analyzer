SUMMARY: This guide teaches how budgets help people plan spending and saving so they can cover needs, pay for wants, and save money regularly. It explains that a budget should be based on net income, which is the amount of money brought home after taxes and other deductions. The document introduces the 50-30-20 budgeting rule as one example of how to divide money among needs, wants, and savings. It also teaches key budgeting vocabulary and uses a station activity with budget cards to help learners practice making spending choices within category limits. The guide emphasizes that the 50-30-20 rule is only one possible rule and that people should use guidelines that fit their own financial situation.

SECTION: Budgeting basics
Knowing how to make a budget is an important step in reaching financial goals. A budget is a plan for how to spend and save money. It helps a person decide how much to spend on necessities, how much to put aside for savings, and how much to use for wants. A budget should be made using net income, which is the amount of money brought home in a paycheck after taxes and other deductions are taken out. A budget should also be reviewed when finances change, such as after getting a new job, moving, or taking out a loan.

SECTION: Why budgets matter
Following a budget helps make sure there is money for things a person needs and wants while still saving regularly. The guide explains that even middle school students can make a budget, such as when managing allowance money or earnings from jobs like babysitting or mowing lawns. Budgeting can help with paying for needed items, spending on enjoyable activities, and saving for future goals such as college or other planned purchases.

SECTION: The 50-30-20 budgeting rule
One budgeting rule presented in the guide is to use 50 percent of income for needs, 30 percent for wants, and 20 percent for savings for financial goals. The guide notes that this is only one rule to live by and that not everyone can follow it. People should decide on a personal rule that works for their own financial situation and use simple spending guidelines that help them reach savings goals.

SECTION: Budget categories and examples

Needs: Rent or mortgage payments, groceries, utilities, transportation, food, medical expenses, car insurance.

Wants: Dining out, vacations, entertainment, a new video game, specialty coffee, cell phone.

Savings: Emergency savings, paying down debt, saving for education, saving for retirement, saving for a large purchase.

SECTION: Monthly budget definition
A monthly budget is a spending plan that outlines how a person will save or spend the money earned that month. The guide organizes budgets into three categories: needs, wants, and savings. It explains that people often create budget rules to help manage money and make decisions about spending within those categories.

SECTION: Key budgeting vocabulary

Budget: A plan that outlines what money a person expects to earn or receive and how that money will be saved or spent for a given period of time; also called a spending plan.

Long-term goals: Goals that can take more than five years to achieve.

Short-term goals: Goals that can take a short time, or up to five years, to reach.

Needs: Basic things people must have to survive, resources they may need to do their jobs, and resources to help build and protect their money and property so they can meet future needs.

Wants: Upgrades and other things that would be nice to have but are not necessary for living, income, or protecting what a person has.

Savings: Money set aside in a secure place, such as a bank account, for future emergencies or specific purchases.

SECTION: Budgeting activity structure
The guide uses an interactive activity in which students work with a budget of 100 points. Their goal is to make spending choices that follow the 50-30-20 rule by spending up to 50 points on needs, 30 points on wants, and 20 points on savings. Students visit needs, wants, and savings stations, choose options from budget cards, record point values, and then answer reflection questions. For this activity, emergency savings is placed in the savings category even though it is often considered a need.

Wants: Card 1 Option A, Card 2 Option B, Card 3 Option A, Card 4 Option A. Savings: Card 1 Option A, Card 2 Option B, Card 3 Option B, Card 4 Option A.

SECTION: Reflection questions and budgeting tradeoffs
The guide asks learners to compare the options they chose with the options they did not choose and think about what those choices might look like in real life. It suggests that living within a budget may require compromises such as living in a smaller or more modest house, having a basic cell phone plan, or planning some meatless meals to save money. It also asks how a budget rule like 50-30-20 might help manage money when living independently, including making sure there is money for needs and wants while still saving regularly.

SECTION: Suggested next steps
The guide suggests using additional Consumer Financial Protection Bureau activities related to saving and spending. Examples named in the guide are “Creating a monthly household budget” and “Brainstorming household budgets.”

ACTIONABLE_GUIDANCE:

Create a budget using net income rather than gross income.

Organize spending into three categories: needs, wants, and savings.

Use the 50-30-20 rule as one possible budgeting guideline: 50 percent for needs, 30 percent for wants, and 20 percent for savings.

Review a budget when financial circumstances change, such as after a new job, a move, or taking out a loan.

Set personal spending guidelines that fit the individual financial situation and support savings goals.

Compare spending choices within each category to decide which options best fit the budget rule.
