TITLE: Bank On It
SOURCE: Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
DOCUMENT_TYPE: curriculum
TOPICS: banking basics, financial institutions, checking accounts, savings accounts, account verification, deposits, withdrawals, balances, bank fees, overdrafts, direct deposit, debit cards, ATM use, online banking, money transfers, privacy notices, opt out rights

SUMMARY: This document teaches core banking and money management concepts for learners who want to use banks and credit unions safely and effectively. It explains why people use banks, how to open and maintain an account, how checking and savings accounts work, and what fees, interest, and balances mean. It also describes common banking services such as direct deposit, ATMs, money orders, online banking, debit cards, money transfers, loans, and stored value cards. The guide includes practical advice on choosing a bank, avoiding unnecessary fees, comparing accounts, protecting personal information, and asking questions before signing up for financial products.

SECTION: What a bank is
A bank is a business that offers a safe place to keep money and uses deposits to make loans. In this curriculum, the term bank also includes credit unions and thrifts. Banks offer many financial services and can help a person build a positive relationship with a financial institution.

SECTION: Why use a bank

Safety: Money is safer from theft, loss, and fire.

Convenience: Money can be accessed quickly and easily, and direct deposit can make funds available sooner than depositing a check.

Cost: Using a bank is probably cheaper than using other businesses to cash checks.

Security: FDIC-insured banks protect deposits up to the maximum amount allowed by law, and most credit unions are insured by NCUA under the same deposit insurance rules.

Financial future: A relationship with a bank can help establish a record of paying bills, help with saving money, and is necessary for getting a loan.

SECTION: Types of financial institutions

Bank: A financial institution run under federal and state laws and regulations that makes loans, pays checks, accepts deposits, and provides other financial services.

Credit union: A non-profit financial institution owned by people who have something in common; membership is required to keep money there.

Thrift: A savings bank or savings and loan association similar to a bank, created to promote home ownership, with most assets in housing-related loans.

SECTION: How to open and maintain a bank account
Opening and maintaining a bank account involves four basic tasks: open the account and complete account verification, make deposits and withdrawals, record interest and fees, and keep track of the account balance. These steps help a person use an account responsibly and avoid unnecessary charges.

SECTION: Account verification and identification
To open a bank account, a person usually goes through account verification. A bank may review checking account history through companies such as TeleCheck or ChexSystems and may also run a full credit report. The bank will need photo identification, such as a driver’s license, and a Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number to verify identity. Some banks may accept other photo identification, such as a matrícula consular card, Green Card, or passport. If a person cannot open an account, the guide recommends asking about second chance checking programs and checking with local financial institutions or reputable credit counseling agencies for available programs.

SECTION: Choosing a bank

Choose a bank that offers the services needed.

Check whether the bank is close to home.

Check whether the bank has reasonable hours.

Check whether the bank has ATMs near where the person lives, works, or shops.

For a credit union, check whether the person is eligible for membership.

Ask whether any employees speak the person’s language.

Ask what fees will be charged.

Confirm that the bank is insured.

SECTION: Deposits, withdrawals, and balances
A deposit is money added to an account, usually using a deposit slip that tells the bank how much money is being added. Depending on whether the deposit is cash, a payroll check, or another type of check, funds may not be available immediately, so the guide says to ask the bank when deposited money can be used. A withdrawal is money taken out of an account by writing a check, using a withdrawal slip, or using an ATM. A balance is the amount of money in the account, and it should be tracked carefully to avoid taking out more than is available.

SECTION: Fees, overdrafts, and interest
Financial institutions charge fees for different services, including monthly maintenance fees and penalty fees for misusing an account. If a person writes a check or makes a transaction for more money than is available, the account is overdrawn and fees may be charged. Some institutions offer courtesy overdraft protection or bounce protection, but these services still charge overdraft or bounce protection fees and do not guarantee that all overdrawn transactions will be covered. Interest is a percentage of the account balance paid by the bank for keeping money there, although not all accounts pay interest.

SECTION: Bank accounts versus check-cashing services
The guide says it is usually much cheaper to use a deposit account at a bank than a check-cashing service. In its example, cashing four checks a month at $5 each costs $240 a year, and buying five money orders a month at $1 each adds $60, for a total yearly cost of $300. By comparison, a checking account with a $5 monthly fee plus $18 for a box of checks costs $78 a year, which is a savings of $222.

SECTION: Checking and savings accounts
A checking account lets a person write checks to pay bills or buy goods, and the financial institution provides a monthly bank statement showing deposits and withdrawals. A savings account always earns interest, generally does not allow check writing, and may require only a few dollars to open, although a monthly fee may be charged if the balance falls below a required amount. Banks may also require a minimum balance to open an account, earn interest, or avoid fees, so comparing account rules is important.

SECTION: Choosing an account

Ask how much money is needed to open the account.

Ask how much money must stay in the account to avoid fees.

Ask what the fees are for bounced checks.

Ask how many checks can be written before extra fees are charged.

Ask how many withdrawals can be made each month.

Ask whether the account pays interest.

Ask whether an ATM card or debit card comes with the account.

Ask whether there are fees for using the ATM or debit card at the same bank or at another bank.

Ask whether there are any other fees.

SECTION: Non-deposit products
Many banks also offer non-deposit products such as stocks, bonds, and mutual funds. These products are not insured by the FDIC and may lose value. The guide advises not buying any product or service that is not understood.

SECTION: Direct deposit
Direct deposit is a method used by an employer or government agency to send a paycheck or benefit payment electronically into an account. The amount is immediately available, and some banks may not charge monthly fees if direct deposit is used. The guide also notes that electronically filing a federal tax return and using direct deposit can make a refund available in 8 to 15 days, while receiving a mailed check can take 6 weeks, and some same-day refund offers come with significant charges.

SECTION: ATM use
An ATM is a kiosk or terminal where a person can deposit, withdraw, or transfer money 24 hours a day. There may be fees for ATM use, especially when using another bank’s ATM, and some deposits may only be possible at the account holder’s own bank ATM. The guide also notes that some banks offer dual ATM cards for family members outside the United States, which may be cheaper than sending a wire transfer.

SECTION: Telephone and online banking
Telephone banking allows a person to check balances by phone, transfer money between accounts, review recent deposits or withdrawals, stop payment on a check, get branch information, and report a lost, stolen, or damaged card. Online banking allows many of the same tasks on a computer, including checking balances, transferring funds, paying bills, submitting a loan application, and sending secure electronic messages to the bank.

SECTION: Money orders, cashier’s checks, and certified checks
A money order is used like a check to pay a bill or make a purchase when cash is not accepted, and many businesses sell money orders for a fee, so the guide recommends shopping around for the best price. A cashier’s check is issued by a financial institution after the person provides the amount of the check plus a service charge, usually $2 to $5, and it is guaranteed not to bounce. A certified check is a personal check marked certified by the bank for a fee, usually $2 to $5, with the money held in the account until the check is processed, and it is also guaranteed not to bounce.

SECTION: Money transfers and remittances
A money transfer is an electronic transfer of money from one bank to another. A remittance is a money transfer sent to a bank or person in another country, and the guide says banks may send money more cheaply than companies such as Western Union or MoneyGram. Before sending a remittance, the guide recommends asking about the total cost including all fees, the total amount the recipient will receive in the destination country, how soon the money will arrive, the background and reputation of the company, whether licensing is required and in place, and whether a receipt or document with all remittance information will be provided.

SECTION: Debit cards
A debit card, sometimes called a check card, is tied to a checking account and removes money from the account immediately when used for purchases. Unlike credit cards, debit cards are described as “buy now, pay now” and do not charge interest, but a person may overdraw the account if debit card transactions are not recorded carefully. If someone uses a debit card without permission, the guide says to inform the bank immediately; if the bank cannot resolve the matter within 10 business days after being told, it will put the money back into the account less $50. A debit card can also function as an ATM card and often requires a PIN, so the guide advises making sure no one can see the PIN when it is entered.

SECTION: Stored value cards
A stored value card is a card that can be loaded with money for future purchases. Examples include telephone cards with prepaid minutes, international gift cards, and payroll cards. The guide says stored value cards may reduce check-cashing fees, provide 24-hour access to funds, make money transfers more available to families, reduce the need to carry cash, and work like a pseudo bank account, but they may also charge many fees, may not be FDIC-insured, and may not protect against loss or theft. Before getting one, the guide recommends asking whether the money is FDIC-insured, what fees will be charged, and what rights apply if the card is lost or stolen.

SECTION: Loans
A loan is money borrowed from a bank with a written promise to repay it later. Banks charge fees and interest for loans, which is the cost of borrowing money.

SECTION: Privacy notices and opt-out rights
Privacy notices explain how a financial company handles, shares, and protects personal financial information. A person receives an initial privacy notice when opening an account and another each year, and federal privacy laws give the right to opt out of some information sharing. To limit promotions or data sharing, the guide says to review the privacy notice for sharing practices and follow the opt-out instructions if they apply. It also says a person can opt out of prescreened credit or insurance offers by calling 1-888-5-OPTOUT or visiting optoutprescreen.com, but some information sharing is still allowed by law and information shared before opting out cannot be retrieved.

SECTION: Bank employees who can help

Customer service representative: Helps open accounts, explains services, answers general questions, refers customers to the right person, and provides written information about bank products.

Teller: Accepts deposits, cashes checks, answers questions, and refers customers for other bank services.

Loan officer: Takes loan applications, answers questions, provides written information about loan products, and helps complete loan applications.

Branch manager: Supervises branch operations and helps solve problems other bank employees cannot solve.

SECTION: Practical banking guidance

Ask the bank what identification is needed before trying to open an account.

Ask whether second chance checking programs are available if an account application is denied.

Ask when deposited funds will be available for use.

Keep track of balances so withdrawals and debit purchases do not overdraw the account.

Compare banks and accounts by fees, minimum balances, ATM access, services, and insurance status.

Do not buy a financial product or service that is not understood.

Shop around for the best price on money orders.

Ask detailed questions before sending a remittance, including fees, exchange rate effects, delivery timing, licensing, and receipt documentation.

Report unauthorized debit card use immediately.

Review privacy notices and use opt-out options when desired.

Ask questions until the information is clear, do not sign anything that is not understood, and ask for written information to review at home.

KEY_TERMS:

bank: A business that offers a safe place to keep money and uses deposits to make loans.

financial institution: A bank, credit union, or thrift that provides financial services.

account verification: The process a bank uses to confirm identity and assess whether a person can open an account.

deposit: Money added to an account.

withdrawal: Money taken out of an account.

balance: The amount of money in a bank account.

overdraft: Taking out or spending more money than is available in the account.

interest: A percentage of the account balance paid by the bank for keeping money there.

checking account: A deposit account that lets a person write checks and make payments.

savings account: A deposit account that earns interest and usually does not allow check writing.

minimum balance: The amount that may be required to open an account, earn interest, or avoid fees.

direct deposit: Electronic deposit of a paycheck or benefit payment into an account.

money transfer: Electronic movement of money from one bank to another.

remittance: A money transfer sent to a bank or person in another country.

debit card: A card tied to a checking account that removes money immediately when used.

stored value card: A card loaded with money for future purchases.

privacy notice: A notice explaining how a financial company collects, shares, and protects personal financial information.

opt out: The right to stop some sharing of personal financial information.

ACTIONABLE_GUIDANCE:

Keep money in an insured bank or credit union for safety, convenience, cost savings, and long-term financial benefits.

Use bank and account checklists to compare services, locations, hours, fees, insurance, ATM access, and minimum balance rules.

Bring acceptable identification and tax identification information when opening an account, and ask what documents are required.

Ask about second chance checking if a regular account cannot be opened.

Track every deposit, withdrawal, fee, and interest payment to maintain an accurate balance.

Avoid overdrawing an account because bounced checks and overdrawn transactions can trigger fees.

Compare the cost of bank accounts with check-cashing services before deciding where to manage money.

Ask about fees before using ATMs, money orders, stored value cards, and other services.

Shop around for the best price on money orders and ask about all remittance fees and exchange-rate effects before sending money internationally.

Report unauthorized debit card use immediately and protect the PIN from being seen by others.

Review privacy notices and follow opt-out instructions if limiting information sharing is important.

Ask questions until terms are clear, ask for written information, and do not sign anything that is not understood.
